About me.

I came to AI through political science. Studying representation and working with constituent opinions in Congress made me attentive to how information is organized, and whose perspective survives that process.

At Columbia, I’ve deepened my skills in computing and machine learning while exploring how AI can be used in public policy. This builds on my background in political science and data analysis, and informs both my research and engineering work. I’m interested in building useful AI systems and understanding how they shape the ways people interpret evidence, make decisions, and work within institutions.

Research interests

Questions I’m exploring+

How can we tell when an AI system is wrong?

A useful evaluation should reveal where a system stops being trustworthy. I’m interested in making failures visible and understanding how they change the conclusions we can responsibly draw.

How does institutional knowledge become a system?

Much of how an organization works is unwritten. Turning that knowledge into an AI workflow requires making definitions, evidence standards, and decision boundaries explicit, then checking them with the people who do the work.

What should we delegate to AI?

Even when a system can perform a task, deciding what to entrust to it remains a human judgment. I’m interested in the boundary between useful delegation, professional expertise, and responsibility.

Who Gets Heeded?

An Obligation-Level Audit of Responsiveness in EPA Rulemaking

Jianing Fan*, Yue Yao*

* Co-first authors; equal contribution.

Read paper ↗ Code & data ↗

Everyone has the formal right to comment on a proposed regulation. But where does that input meet the actual text of a rule?

We built an LLM-assisted pipeline to extract regulatory obligations, match public comments, and compare proposed and final rule text. Blind human audits test the measurements behind the findings.

My contributionI formulated the research question and led the research.

The central question: who has the capacity to identify, interpret, and contest a regulatory obligation?

Reading the result

Engagement accompanies revision.
That does not establish influence.

The pooled revision rate is higher for obligations addressed by at least five commenters. Once comparisons are made within rulemakings, the estimate is statistically inconclusive.

This is descriptive evidence, not a causal claim about whose comments changed a rule.

Figure 2, upper panel From our September 28 manuscript · CC BY 4.0.

Read the findings and measurement limits+
What we find

Participation is more than access.

Greater engagement co-occurs with more revision across rulemakings. Supporting versus opposing comments do not clearly differentiate outcomes. Under a heuristic classification of commenter type, organizational-majority engagement is more common among editorially refined obligations than substantively modified ones.

What needs care

The measurement is part of the result.

A blind audit exposed low classifier–human agreement (κ = .137) when distinguishing editorial from substantive change. We recomputed the relevant finding with human-adjudicated labels. The commenter-type proxy remains unaudited, and within-rulemaking associations remain inconclusive.
